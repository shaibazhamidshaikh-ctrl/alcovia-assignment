Product, Systems & Analytics Lead: how I'd run the first six weeks

Shaibaz Shaikh · Assignment submission · Team: me + 1 full-stack engineer (FSE) + 1 process/systems designer (SD) · Start date 1 Nov 2026

  1. One data spine first. All four problems fail for the same reason: data lives in ClickUp notes, Razorpay, Meta and the app, and nobody can see it together. Weeks 1–2 build one Supabase warehouse fed by n8n, so every dashboard after that is a view, not a project.
  2. ClickUp stays the team's UI. I'm not replacing the tool the team already uses. Every ClickUp status change becomes a timestamped event, which is what makes show-up rate and journey length computable instead of typed.
  3. App adoption: I'd change the target from "30 minutes in the app" to "45 focus-minutes a day, with a study task attached." The Realm already holds attention, so the plan is to route study through it rather than build more features. Of the 25 features, about 8 matter right now.
  4. Delivery: no feature starts without a one-page brief stating the metric, baseline, target and kill rule, and nothing is "done" until its tracking events fire. Each launch gets a readout at day 14 where we decide to scale, iterate or kill.
  5. Student dashboard: every activity is measured on three lenses (did we run it, did they like it, did they improve), with data captured automatically. The only manual inputs are a 1-tap session rating and one facilitator form per workshop.

2. App adoption: from 2.15 minutes to a habit

What the data says

  • Students come for Home (all) and the Realm (13 of 20). Focus Timer is opened by 11 but kept by 5. Wellness, Streak, Rankings and Goals are effectively unused.
  • Study features fade: 10 opened Syllabus, 1 this week; nobody opened Planner or Academic history this week. The study layer sits next to the game instead of being inside it.
  • The base is 20 students and 7 are dormant. At this size, cohort A/B tests are meaningless; I'd use before/after per student, plus conversations.

Reframing the target

30 minutes a day of screen time in a study app can be hit by a game loop alone, which helps no parent. I'd propose a north star of focus-minutes per active student per day (target 45), where each focus session is attached to a chapter or task. Because focus sessions run inside the app, hitting 45 focus minutes delivers the 30-minute app-time goal as a by-product, honestly.

Input metrics: weekly active students (target 18 of 20), % of days with the 8 PM quiz done in-app, % of focus sessions linked to a chapter, and the count of chapters moving up a rigour stage per week.

What I saw using the app (test account, 23 Oct)
  1. Focus works, but nothing else gets credit for it. Today's Focus shows 85 minutes across 10 sessions. On the same Home screen, Daily Velocity is 0.0%, tasks are 0/4, Study Planner shows 0h completed of 59h, and the streak is 0 days. A student who did the right thing sees four failures. This is why bet 2 links each focus session to a chapter or task.
  2. The task list is a backlog, not today's mission. "Today's Mission" opens on All Tasks: 104 of 156 done with 51 overdue, led by May and June items. Many are goal-sized ("Improve discipline and time management") rather than doable today. The October monthly goals all say "No daily tasks planned yet," so the roadmap never turns into daily work.
  3. Progress numbers disagree. Home shows syllabus at 13% (7/52 chapters); Study shows 6% (8/144). Math (0/34) and Mathematics (1/4) are separate subjects. Physics shows 19% with 0/4 chapters done. Study Planner says "On track" at 0h of 59h. If the student can't trust the numbers, an advisor can't either. This is the case for one readiness definition (the 5 rigour stages).
  4. Manual logging doesn't happen. Daily Habit Analysis shows 16.9h as "Other" and 0h for school and self-study, then builds insights on that. Data that needs the teen to type it will stay empty, which is why the student dashboard pulls from focus sessions, tasks and wearables instead.
  5. Too many places, rewards without meaning. Explore lists 16 features in four groups, and the balance shows 202,688 coins. At that scale coins stop feeling like a reward. Notifications are off, and the permission prompt appears on open and leads with "castle under attack." I'd ask after the first completed focus session and lead with the 8 PM quiz and session reminders.

4. Structure that makes features actually work

The failure pattern I'd design against: features ship, nobody checks whether they moved anything, and the next feature starts. Every OKR, whether an app feature, an automation or an analytics upload, goes through the same seven steps.

1 · FrameMe. One-page brief: user, problem, success metric with baseline and target, guardrail metric, kill rule.
2 · ShapeSD + me. Flow on paper, cognitive-load check (one primary action per screen, ≤3 taps), events to track named before build.
3 · BuildFSE. 1-week slices. Done = works + events fire + n8n failures route to the error flow.
4 · QAMe. Test against the brief on real devices and the test account; data check vs source totals.
5 · LaunchStaged: 3 students → all. Advisors briefed; parent message if it touches them.
6 · ReadoutDay 7 early check, day 14 decision: scale, iterate or kill. Auto-built from the dashboards.
7 · LogOne line in the decision log: what we learned and what we stop doing.

Rhythm

  • Monday (45 min): plan the week from the OKR board in ClickUp; each card links its brief.
  • Daily: async ClickUp update (done / doing / blocked). A call only when something is blocked.
  • Friday (45 min): demo what shipped plus metric review of anything past day 7.
  • Monthly: retro and OKR score. The team metric is % of launched OKRs that hit their success metric (start target 50%, then 65%). This rewards fewer, better bets over shipping volume.

Guardrails that stop systems breaking

  • A metric dictionary owned by me: one definition per KPI, used by the SQL, the dashboards and the team.
  • Every automation is idempotent (source ids as keys) and wired to the error-handler flow, which turns failures into a P1 ClickUp task.
  • A weekly reconciliation: warehouse revenue vs Razorpay settlement, lead count vs Meta lead forms. A gap over 2% blocks the Monday review.
  • A ClickUp status contract: statuses can't be renamed without updating the flow 03 map (owned by the SD).

Scope for advisors in non-tuition subjects

Advisors aren't subject experts, so they own the process, not the content: setting targets from the affinity × ability matrix, enforcing the 5-stage rigour (read → notes → practice ≥70% → timed past papers → ≥80% ready), protecting self-study slots in the timetable, and escalating to a tutor when the data says so. The dashboard gives them exactly those levers and nothing that needs subject knowledge.

Where and when: Metabase on the same warehouse, embedded in ClickUp, live for the weekly review by 12 Dec (weeks 3–6 above). A monthly parent progress card is generated from the same data.

6. Risks and what I'm not doing yet

  • n = 20. Every "metric moved" claim is read alongside conversations; no fake statistical significance.
  • Teen data privacy. Wearable and screen-time data need explicit parent consent and minimal retention; parents see summaries, not raw sleep logs.
  • Not building yet: a custom CRM (ClickUp is fine), AI timetable generation (needs 6 weeks of clean timetable data first), and Wellness features (wait for ≥5 connected wearables).
